The Quiet Return of Passive Cooling

A For most of the twentieth century, the story of thermal comfort in buildings was a story of machines. The invention of modern air conditioning in 1902 by Willis Carrier — originally designed to control humidity in a Brooklyn printing plant — gradually transformed architecture itself. Buildings no longer needed to respond to their climates; they could simply be sealed and mechanically cooled. By the 1960s, the glass-box office tower, identical whether built in Stockholm or Singapore, had become the international symbol of modernity. What was lost in this transition was several millennia of accumulated knowledge about how to keep buildings cool without consuming energy.

B That knowledge was extensive. Traditional builders in hot climates developed remarkably sophisticated techniques long before electricity existed. In the Persian Gulf, wind towers known as badgirs channelled breezes down into living quarters while allowing warm air to escape, sometimes passing incoming air over pools of water to cool it through evaporation. In Southeast Asia, houses were raised on stilts to catch ground-level breezes, with steep roofs and deep overhangs that shaded walls from the high tropical sun while shedding monsoon rain. Courtyard houses across the Mediterranean and Middle East used thick masonry walls to absorb heat during the day and release it during cooler nights, a strategy engineers now call thermal mass. None of these techniques required a single watt of electricity.

C The economic logic of abandoning such methods once seemed unassailable. Air conditioning was cheap to run when energy was cheap, and it offered something passive techniques could not: precise, guaranteed control. A developer could promise tenants twenty-three degrees regardless of what the weather did. Passive design, by contrast, could only promise comfort most of the time, and its performance depended on correct orientation, careful detailing and sometimes on occupants' willingness to open and close windows at appropriate hours. In a commercial property market that valued predictability, the machine won.

D Yet the costs of that victory have become impossible to ignore. Cooling now accounts for nearly ten percent of global electricity consumption, and the International Energy Agency projects that the number of air conditioning units worldwide could triple by 2050, driven largely by rising incomes in hot, populous countries such as India, Indonesia and Nigeria. This creates a feedback loop with troubling implications: air conditioners expel waste heat into city streets, raising outdoor temperatures — studies in Tokyo and Paris have measured night-time increases of one to two degrees attributable to air conditioning exhaust — which in turn increases the demand for more cooling.

E In response, a growing movement of architects and engineers is attempting not to abandon mechanical cooling but to reduce buildings' dependence on it. The approach is sometimes called "passive first": design the building envelope, orientation and ventilation so effectively that machines are needed only during the most extreme conditions. In Ahmedabad, India, a hospital completed in 2014 combined thick insulated walls, small carefully-shaded windows and night-time ventilation to keep indoor temperatures roughly ten degrees below outdoor peaks without any air conditioning in its general wards. In Singapore, several recent high-rise developments have revived the deep balconies, sky gardens and cross-ventilation of the region's vernacular shophouses, cutting cooling loads by margins their developers were initially reluctant to believe.

F Technology, ironically, has accelerated the revival of these pre-technological ideas. Computer simulation now allows designers to model airflow, solar radiation and thermal behaviour before construction begins, converting what was once inherited craft knowledge into quantifiable engineering. A designer in Bangkok can test forty roof configurations in an afternoon — an experimental luxury unavailable to the anonymous builders who refined the traditional Thai house over centuries of trial and error. Some researchers argue this represents the ideal synthesis: ancient strategies validated and optimised by modern analysis.

G Obstacles remain, however, and they are less technical than institutional. Building codes in many countries were written around mechanical systems and can actually penalise naturally ventilated designs. Property valuations rarely reward low running costs as strongly as they punish unconventional appearance. And the construction industry, like any industry, prefers to repeat what it already knows how to build. The techniques that kept people comfortable for five thousand years are no longer secret; whether they become standard practice again is a question of incentives rather than knowledge.

Questions 1–5 · True / False / Not Given

Write TRUE if the statement agrees with the passage, FALSE if it contradicts it, NOT GIVEN if there is no information.

Q1✓ Correct✗ Wrong

Air conditioning was originally invented to cool office workers.

FALSE. Paragraph A: it was "originally designed to control humidity in a Brooklyn printing plant" — for machines/paper, not office workers. A stated purpose that differs from the statement = contradiction, not absence of information.
Q2✓ Correct✗ Wrong

Badgirs sometimes used water to lower the temperature of incoming air.

TRUE. Paragraph B: "sometimes passing incoming air over pools of water to cool it through evaporation." Direct paraphrase — "sometimes" in both.
Q3✓ Correct✗ Wrong

Passive design techniques guaranteed the same level of comfort as air conditioning.

FALSE. Paragraph C: AC offered "precise, guaranteed control" while passive design "could only promise comfort most of the time". The passage explicitly contrasts them — a direct contradiction of "guaranteed the same level".
Q4✓ Correct✗ Wrong

The IEA predicts most new air conditioning demand by 2050 will come from Europe.

FALSE. Paragraph D: growth is "driven largely by rising incomes in hot, populous countries such as India, Indonesia and Nigeria" — the passage names where most demand comes from, and it is not Europe. Contradiction, not NOT GIVEN.
Q5✓ Correct✗ Wrong

The Ahmedabad hospital uses no air conditioning anywhere in the building.

NOT GIVEN. Paragraph E says no AC "in its general wards" — a limited claim. Whether other areas (operating theatres, offices) use AC is simply not stated. Classic trap: a TRUE answer over-extends the passage's claim; FALSE requires contradiction that isn't there.
